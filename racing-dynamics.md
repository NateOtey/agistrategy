**Definition:** Competitive pressure that pushes AI developers, or countries, to move faster and cut corners on safety, often when each of them would prefer to slow down.

**Example or analogy:** In 1939, Leo Szilard and Albert Einstein warned President Roosevelt that Nazi Germany might build an atomic bomb. Fear that Germany would get there first drove the Manhattan Project forward at enormous speed, and many scientists set aside their moral misgivings because the alternative seemed worse. By late 1944, it had become clear that the German program was nowhere close. The original justification had evaporated, yet the project pressed on. [Joseph Rotblat](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/people/joseph-rotblat/) left on grounds of conscience, and he is usually described as the only scientist who did. (Races, it turns out, are easier to join than to leave.)

**Relevance to strategy:** Racing can convert good intentions into bad outcomes. A lab that sincerely believes it is the most careful developer can reason its way to the conclusion that it must stay ahead, which speeds up the race for everyone. The US–China rivalry raises the stakes, since any proposal to slow down can be framed as handing an advantage to a rival. In 2024, the US–China Economic and Security Review Commission went so far as to [recommend](https://www.eweek.com/de/news/bipartisan-manhattan-project-for-ai-development/) that Congress fund a "Manhattan Project-style" program to develop AGI. Much of AI strategy, e.g. export controls, international agreements, and pacing proposals, amounts to an attempt either to win the race safely or to change its rules. Useful work here includes making safety cheaper so that racing costs less, building tools that make agreements verifiable, and designing policies that change the incentives.

**Where people disagree:** How much of a race there actually is. Some argue that "the AI race" is partly a story told to justify recklessness. On this view, a rival may be further behind than feared, as Germany was, and cooperation between the US and China is more feasible than the rhetoric suggests. Others think the competition is well underway and that losing it would be catastrophic.
