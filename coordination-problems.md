**Definition:** Situations in which everyone would be better off cooperating, but each individual actor has a reason not to.

**Example or analogy:** The classic case is the prisoner's dilemma. Two suspects are arrested and questioned in separate rooms. Each can stay silent or betray the other. If both stay silent, each serves one year. If one betrays and the other stays silent, the betrayer goes free and the silent one serves ten years. If both betray, each serves five. Whatever the other suspect does, betraying is the better move for you, so both betray and both serve five years, a worse outcome than the one they would have gotten by trusting each other. (Note that the suspects need not dislike each other; the trap works perfectly well between friends.) Now replace the suspects with two AI labs, and replace "stay silent" with "take the time to make our model safe."

**Relevance to strategy:** Many AI risks are coordination problems first and technical problems second. Even if researchers knew exactly how to build AI safely, competitive pressure could keep developers from doing so. This explains why individual good actors cannot solve the problem alone, and why success may depend as much on institutions, treaties, and trust as on research. The usual ways out of a prisoner's dilemma all have AI versions: agreements that bind everyone at once, ways to verify that the other side is keeping its word (see *Compute governance*), repeated interactions that reward a reputation for cooperation, and outside enforcement by governments.

**Where people disagree:** Whether coordination on AI is achievable. Optimists point to precedents such as nuclear arms control and the Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting chemicals. Pessimists note that AI is harder to verify, more economically valuable, and moving faster than any of those. A further debate concerns which coordination is desirable at all, since an agreement among a few powerful actors can slide into a dangerous concentration of power.
