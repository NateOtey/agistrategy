**Definition:** Legal responsibility for harm. In AI governance, the question is who must pay, and how much, when an AI system causes damage: the developer, the company that deployed it, the user, or no one. Most of the relevant law falls under torts, the branch of law that governs compensation for injuries.

**Example or analogy:** A company that uses dynamite to clear a construction site is liable for damage the blast causes, even if it took every reasonable precaution. Courts call this "strict liability" and reserve it for abnormally dangerous activities, on the theory that whoever chooses to do something risky should bear its costs. The legal scholar Gabriel Weil argues that training and deploying frontier AI should be treated similarly, and that courts should be able to award extra damages in cases that came close to catastrophe, so that companies pay for risks they impose on everyone else.

**Relevance to strategy:** Liability makes developers weigh the costs of harm without requiring regulators to anticipate every specific danger in advance, which is an advantage when the technology is changing faster than rules can be written. It harnesses existing courts and insurance markets, and it can be adopted by states or countries acting alone. Its main limitation is timing: liability works after the fact, and the worst AI harms, such as a global catastrophe, could not be compensated afterward because there might be no one left to sue, or nothing left to pay with. (Few legal remedies are designed for the end of the world.) Weil himself argues that liability must therefore be paired with other measures.

**Where people disagree:** Whether liability would chill beneficial innovation or merely make companies price in the risks they create, who should be liable when many parties contribute to a harm, and whether liability rules should shield open-source developers.
