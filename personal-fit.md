**Definition:** Personal fit is how good you are likely to be at a particular kind of work, compared with the typical person who does it. Comparative advantage is what you can contribute relative to the other people available to do it: not what you are best at in absolute terms, but where you add most compared with whoever would otherwise fill the role. Career capital is the skills, credentials, relationships and reputation that make you more useful later.

**Example or analogy:** A mid-career employment lawyer who becomes worried about AI might assume the important thing to do is retrain as a machine learning researcher. But the field has many strong ML candidates and few people who can draft legislation, read a liability regime or negotiate with a general counsel. Her comparative advantage probably lies in AI policy or law, using the expertise she already has, and she may get there in months instead of years. An orchestra short of oboists gains more from a competent oboist than from another excellent violinist.

**Relevance to strategy:** Differences in fit are large: the most effective people in a role often contribute many times more than the median. That makes "which problem should I work on?" only half the question; "where would I personally be unusually good?" is the other half. It also changes as a field matures. As AI safety grows, the scarcest skills shift, from early researchers toward operators, policy experts, security engineers, communicators and founders who can run organizations.

**Where people disagree:** How far to trust your own sense of fit, given that people are often wrong about what they would be good at, and how much to rely instead on cheap tests such as short projects, courses and fellowships. Whether to build general career capital first or move into the field now, given short timelines: waiting may make you more effective, but some argue the most important decisions will be made before you are ready.
