**Definition:** Agreements and institutions among countries for managing AI risks. They range from summits and shared testing standards to treaties that would limit or pace frontier development, ideally backed by ways to verify that each side is keeping its word.

**Example or analogy:** The closest precedent is nuclear arms control. In 1946, the United States proposed the Baruch Plan to place atomic energy under international control; it failed, but decades later the Non-Proliferation Treaty and international inspections of nuclear facilities did manage to slow the spread of nuclear weapons. AI efforts so far are more modest. At the 2023 Bletchley Park summit, 28 countries and the European Union signed a declaration recognizing the risks of frontier AI. Proposals for stronger arrangements include Cullen O'Keefe's "Chips for Peace," in which the US and its allies would pair shared safety rules with sharing the benefits of AI and restricting the spread of dangerous capabilities, and a system of international certification in which countries' AI regulations are checked against common standards.

**Relevance to strategy:** Racing between countries is a coordination problem that no single government can solve on its own. Many of the most ambitious AI strategies, including the AI Futures Project's "Plan A," depend on some form of US–China agreement. The crux is usually verification: whether each side can confirm what the other is building, which is why compute governance, chip tracking, and data center monitoring feature so heavily in proposals.

**Where people disagree:** Whether substantive agreement between the US and China is possible in the current political climate, whether verification technology can mature in time, and whether international institutions would end up too weak to matter or powerful enough to become a risk themselves.
