**Definition:** Knowledge, technology, or capabilities that can serve both beneficial and harmful ends.

**Example or analogy:** In 2022, a company that uses AI to discover new medicines ran a small experiment for a conference on chemical weapons. Its software normally steers away from toxic molecules. The researchers simply flipped the setting to reward toxicity instead, and in under six hours the model proposed around 40,000 candidate molecules, including the known nerve agent VX and many compounds predicted to be even more lethal. Nothing about the model had changed except the direction it was pointed. The authors described the result as a wake-up call for their own field.

**Relevance to strategy:** Most of the AI capabilities that worry safety researchers are dual use. Biological knowledge that helps design vaccines also helps design pathogens; the skill that lets an AI find software vulnerabilities for defenders lets it find them for attackers; persuasive ability serves teachers and propagandists alike. This rules out simple bans on whole domains of knowledge, which would sacrifice enormous benefits, and pushes strategy toward narrower tools: restricting specific high-risk capabilities, controlling who can access them, screening dangerous requests (such as orders for DNA synthesis), and investing in defenses that let the beneficial uses outpace the harmful ones.

**Where people disagree:** Where to draw the line between information that should flow freely and information that should be restricted, and whether restrictions on AI models do much good when the same knowledge is available elsewhere. These disagreements usually turn on uplift: how much the AI adds beyond what a determined bad actor could already find.
