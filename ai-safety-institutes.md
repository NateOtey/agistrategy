**Definition:** Government bodies created to test frontier AI systems, research their risks, and advise policymakers. The United Kingdom launched the first in November 2023, followed by the United States, Japan, Singapore, Canada, and others, along with the European Union's AI Office. As of October 2026, several have been renamed to emphasize security and competitiveness: the UK body became the AI Security Institute in 2025, and the US body became the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI).

**Example or analogy:** Think of a national testing laboratory, such as those that test car safety or check food and medicine, but for AI. Most AI Safety Institutes, however, lack the power to block a product; they test models with the developers' cooperation, publish research, and advise governments. (Imagine a food inspector who can taste anything in the kitchen by invitation, and whose report the restaurant is free to frame or to ignore.)

**Relevance to strategy:** These institutes are where governments build their own technical understanding of frontier AI, instead of relying entirely on what companies tell them. They run pre-deployment evaluations of new models, develop testing methods, and share findings across countries through an international network. Their staff and expertise also make them natural homes for future regulatory functions, should lawmakers create them. The renamings reflect shifting political priorities, and they illustrate how dependent these institutions are on the governments of the day.

**Where people disagree:** Whether the institutes should gain binding powers, how independent they can be when they rely on companies' voluntary cooperation, and whether a focus on national security strengthens their mission or narrows it.
