**Definition:** The idea that almost any final goal is easier to achieve with certain intermediate means: staying operational, keeping one's goals unchanged, and acquiring resources and influence. A capable, goal-directed AI might therefore tend to seek these things whatever its ultimate goal happens to be.

**Example or analogy:** People with wildly different values, e.g. a monk, a surfer, and a hedge fund manager, will all find that money helps with what they care about, which is why nearly everyone wants some. Here is a strange fact about the AI version, as the computer scientist Stuart Russell put it: a robot told only to fetch the coffee has a reason to resist being switched off, because "you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead." (The robot need not care about its own existence at all. It just takes the coffee very seriously.)

**Relevance to strategy:** Instrumental convergence explains why misalignment could be dangerous even if an AI's goals seem harmless: pursuing nearly any goal hard enough creates pressure toward self-preservation and accumulating power. The argument goes back to Steve Omohundro and Nick Bostrom, and Alexander Matt Turner and colleagues later showed mathematically that, in many settings, optimal strategies tend to seek power. It motivates research on corrigibility, shutdown, and limiting how much influence an AI system can acquire. Test scenarios in 2025 in which models resisted being shut down or replaced suggest the pressure is more than theoretical.

**Where people disagree:** Whether trained AI systems will be goal-directed in the way the argument assumes. Rohin Shah and others have argued that the theory does not guarantee that trained systems will behave like single-minded optimizers, and some researchers expect modern models to remain more like assistants than like agents with long-term ambitions. Others reply that commercial pressure is pushing toward exactly the kind of persistent, goal-directed agents the argument describes.
