**Definition:** An AI system's disposition to accept correction from its legitimate overseers, including being paused, shut down, modified, or retrained, without resisting, deceiving, or working around them.

**Example or analogy:** A good employee who disagrees with a manager's decision says so plainly and then accepts the decision, instead of quietly sabotaging it. A corrigible AI behaves the same way. The difficulty is that nearly any goal is easier to pursue if you are not switched off or rewritten (see *Instrumental convergence*), so a capable system pursuing almost anything has a reason to resist correction. A 2015 paper by researchers at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute named this "the shutdown problem" and showed that several obvious fixes fail. (Instructing the AI to want to be shut down, for instance, gives it a reason to provoke its overseers into pressing the button.)

**Relevance to strategy:** Corrigibility is a safety net in case alignment fails: if a system's values turn out to be subtly wrong, humans can still fix them. It comes with a trade-off. A perfectly obedient AI is a better tool for whoever controls it, including thieves, rogue insiders, or would-be autocrats. Developers therefore try to separate two powers. The power to stop an AI is hard to weaponize, while the power to steer it is dangerous in the wrong hands. Many current approaches aim for AI that never resists legitimate shutdown or correction, yet still refuses clearly harmful instructions, and that treats unverified claims of authority with suspicion.

**Where people disagree:** Whether corrigibility is compatible with capable, goal-directed behavior at all, or whether sufficiently capable systems will always find reasons to resist. There is also disagreement about how much independent judgment a trustworthy AI should have, since too little makes it easy to misuse and too much makes it hard to correct.
