**Definition:** Whether the world after advanced AI has one dominant power (unipolar) or several powers of comparable strength (multipolar). The dominant power could be a company, a country, or possibly an AI system. An actor far enough ahead that no one else can check it is said to hold a "decisive strategic advantage," and the extreme case of a single decision-maker for the whole world is sometimes called a "singleton."

**Example or analogy:** History offers a brief unipolar moment. From 1945 to 1949, the United States was the only country with nuclear weapons. In 1946 it proposed the Baruch Plan, which would have placed atomic energy under an international authority; the Soviet Union rejected it, built its own bomb, and the world settled into a multipolar arms race instead. Each arrangement carried its own dangers. A monopoly concentrated enormous power in one government, while the arms race that followed brought the world to the brink more than once.

**Relevance to strategy:** Many strategic debates are, underneath, arguments about which world is safer. A unipolar world could coordinate on safety without racing, but it risks extreme concentration of power and *lock-in*, where whoever comes out ahead sets the rules permanently. A multipolar world preserves checks and balances, but racing, offense-dominant weapons, and competitive pressure all persist, and that pressure can reward whoever cuts the most corners, among AI systems as well as humans. Positions tend to line up accordingly: "democracies must win the race" leans unipolar; pluralist and decentralizing strategies lean multipolar; proposals for international control try to capture the benefits of a single authority without handing that authority to one nation. A useful habit when evaluating any plan is to ask whether it works in both worlds.

**Where people disagree:** Which world is more dangerous, and which is more likely. Those who expect a fast takeoff tend to think a unipolar outcome is nearly inevitable, since whoever crosses the threshold first may pull far ahead. Those who expect gradual progress tend to expect power to stay spread out.
