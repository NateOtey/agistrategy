**Definition:** The view, set out by Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor in 2025, that AI is best understood as a powerful general-purpose technology, comparable to electricity or the internet, whose effects will unfold gradually over decades. On this view, AI is unlikely to become an autonomous superintelligence that escapes human control, and policy should focus on resilience and reducing uncertainty instead of drastic interventions.

**Example or analogy:** Electric motors were available in the 1880s, yet the economic historian Paul David found that factories took roughly four decades to deliver large productivity gains from them. The delay had little to do with the technology itself. Factories had been designed around a single central steam engine, and to take advantage of electricity they had to be rebuilt from the floor plan up, along with new management practices and newly trained workers. Narayanan and Kapoor argue that AI will follow a similar path: the bottleneck lies in how slowly organizations, laws, and people adapt, however quickly capabilities improve in the lab.

**Relevance to strategy:** This view is the most developed intellectual counterweight to the assumptions behind much of the course, and taking it seriously sharpens any strategy. If AI's impact is gradual, the world will have time to observe problems and respond, which favors adaptive policies such as transparency, monitoring, and strengthening institutions over extraordinary measures such as pauses or international control regimes. Narayanan and Kapoor also warn that drastic interventions aimed at hypothetical superintelligence could cause harm of their own, e.g. by concentrating power or stifling beneficial uses.

**Where people disagree:** The central crux is speed. Critics argue that AI differs from past technologies because it can improve itself and do the work of adapting organizations, which would break the historical pattern of slow diffusion. Defenders reply that this argument has been made about many technologies before, and that real-world deployment keeps proving slower and messier than forecasts suggest.
