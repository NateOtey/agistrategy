**Definition:** Frontier safety frameworks are policies in which AI companies commit to specific safety measures once their models reach specified capability thresholds. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP, 2023) was the first; OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework followed. Third-party compliance reviews are independent checks on whether a company is actually following its own framework.

**Example or analogy:** Biology labs operate under biosafety levels, from BSL-1 for harmless microbes to BSL-4 for the deadliest pathogens, with stricter containment at each level. Anthropic modeled its "AI Safety Levels" on the same idea: as a model's dangerous capabilities increase, so do the required security and deployment safeguards. If the safeguards are not ready, the commitment is to pause scaling or deployment until they are. (A promise is easier to keep when someone else is checking, hence the interest in third-party reviews.)

**Relevance to strategy:** For several years, these frameworks were the main form of governance at the frontier. At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, sixteen companies committed to publish such frameworks, and California's SB 53 (2025) now requires large frontier developers to publish theirs. They have clear strengths: they tie safety to measurable evidence, and they spread norms across companies. They also have weaknesses. Companies write and revise their own thresholds, interpret their own eval results, and could loosen commitments under competitive pressure. Independent compliance reviews, public reporting, and legal requirements are attempts to make the commitments more credible.

**Where people disagree:** Whether voluntary frameworks hold up under racing pressure, or mainly serve to delay binding regulation. Supporters see them as a practical bridge to regulation and a source of tested ideas for lawmakers; critics note that several companies have weakened or reinterpreted their frameworks over time.
