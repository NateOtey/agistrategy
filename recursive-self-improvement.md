**Definition:** Three linked ideas about AI accelerating its own progress.

- **Automated AI R&D:** AI systems doing much of the work of AI researchers, e.g. writing training code, running experiments, and proposing new methods. This is the near-term, measurable version.
- **Recursive self-improvement:** AI improving the process that produces AI, so that each generation helps build the next.
- **Intelligence explosion:** the hypothesized result if each improvement speeds up the next one, producing a rapid, runaway rise in capability. The idea goes back to the statistician I. J. Good, who wrote in 1965 that the first "ultraintelligent machine" would be "the last invention that man need ever make."

**Example or analogy:** Compound interest is the familiar version: money earns interest, and the interest earns interest. Now imagine a research lab that, instead of hiring a few new scientists each year, can copy its best researcher a thousand times overnight, and whose copies then help design a better researcher to copy. (Human researchers, by contrast, require sleep, coffee, and occasionally tenure.)

**Relevance to strategy:** If AI research itself is automated, everything downstream speeds up, including the time available to notice and fix problems. This is why the July 2026 Pacing the Frontier statement, signed by more than 1,300 employees of frontier AI companies, focuses specifically on "automated AI development." It is also why labs have begun publishing measurements of how much AI contributes to their own research, and why forecasters treat AI R&D capabilities as the most important threshold to watch. Proposals to monitor, pace, or pause frontier development generally target this feedback loop.

**Where people disagree:** Whether bottlenecks will cap the loop. Skeptics argue that experiments still need scarce compute, that some progress requires slow real-world testing, and that ideas get harder to find, so automated research would speed things up without producing an explosion. Others argue that once AI can do most research tasks, these bottlenecks would themselves be attacked by AI, and progress could compress decades into a few years.
