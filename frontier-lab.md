**Definition:** A frontier model is one of the most capable AI systems in existence at a given moment. A frontier lab is an organization that builds them.

**Example or analogy:** Think of Formula 1. Only a handful of teams can afford to compete at the front of the grid, their cars run on technology years ahead of anything in a showroom, and today's racing innovation becomes tomorrow's family sedan feature. The AI frontier moves the same way, only faster: a model that defined the frontier two years ago may now run on a laptop.

**Relevance to strategy:** Most AI safety policy targets the frontier, because that is where new and potentially dangerous capabilities appear first. It is also where resources concentrate, since only a few organizations can afford the computing power needed to train frontier models. This makes the frontier a natural chokepoint for oversight, and the small number of players makes coordination more tractable than in most industries. It also makes those few companies extraordinarily influential. Many safety roles sit inside frontier labs, on alignment, security, and policy teams; others sit outside them, in evaluation, auditing, and regulation. As of October 2026, the frontier is usually understood to include a few US companies and a growing number of Chinese ones.

**Where people disagree:** How to define the frontier for legal purposes: by the computing power used in training, by measured capabilities, or by cost. Each definition can be gamed, and each goes stale as the technology improves. A further debate concerns whether a focus on the frontier neglects risks from smaller models that are cheaper, widely available, and harder to control once released.
