**Definition:** Field-building is work that increases the number and quality of people working on a problem, as opposed to working on the problem directly. A talent pipeline is the sequence of steps that takes someone from first hearing about a problem to working on it full-time: typically an introductory course, then a fellowship or bootcamp, then a job, grant or new organization.

**Example or analogy:** A common path in AI safety runs from an online course such as BlueDot's AGI Strategy course, to a mentored fellowship such as MATS or the GovAI fellowship, to a job at a safety organization, a government AI institute or a lab's safety team. The field has grown quickly this way: one count found about 1,100 people working full-time at AI safety organizations in 2025, up from about 400 in 2022, not counting most of the frontier labs' own safety teams.

**Relevance to strategy:** If the binding constraint on making AI go well is people rather than money, then building the pipeline may be among the highest-leverage things anyone can do, and every step of it matters: who hears about the problem, who gets selected, what they read first, and where they are pointed next. Pipelines also shape the field's priorities, since the problems newcomers are taught to care about are the ones they go on to work on.

**Where people disagree:** What the bottleneck actually is. Some argue the field badly needs more people, especially in policy, operations, security and founding new organizations. Others reply that fellowships already reject most applicants and that experienced mentors, not willing newcomers, are what is scarce; an October 2026 LessWrong post went further, arguing that there are far more talented people trying to enter AI safety than there are jobs, and that urging people to pivot can hurt them. A related dispute is whether pipelines that feed people into frontier labs make those labs safer or simply staff the race.
