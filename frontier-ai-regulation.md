**Definition:** Laws aimed specifically at the most capable AI models and the companies that build them, as distinct from rules about particular uses of AI such as hiring or lending.

**Example or analogy:** As of October 2026, two of the most prominent examples are:

- **The EU AI Act:** in force since August 2024, with obligations for general-purpose AI models beginning in August 2025. Models trained with more than 10^25 calculations are presumed to pose "systemic risk" and face extra requirements, such as evaluations, incident reporting, and cybersecurity measures.
- **California's SB 53:** signed in September 2025, the first US state law aimed specifically at frontier AI safety. It requires large frontier developers to publish safety frameworks, report critical safety incidents, and protect whistleblowers.

Much frontier regulation so far emphasizes transparency (telling the public and government what companies are doing) over direct restrictions on what they may build. Lawmakers, it seems, prefer to start by asking companies to show their homework before deciding whether to grade it.

**Relevance to strategy:** Regulation converts voluntary commitments into binding ones, which matters most when competitive pressure tempts companies to cut corners. Because AI is global, regulation also raises questions of jurisdiction: whether rules in one place shape behavior everywhere (as EU rules often do), whether US states or the federal government should set the rules, and how to avoid simply pushing development elsewhere. Strategists also weigh the timing of regulation, since rules written too early may target the wrong things, while rules written too late may arrive after the decisive developments.

**Where people disagree:** How much regulation frontier AI needs now, and of what kind. Some argue that transparency and liability are enough for the moment; others argue for licensing, mandatory safety cases, or government authority to block deployments. Skeptics, including Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan, question whether AI risks justify extraordinary government intervention at all.
