**Definition:** A situation in which humans can no longer effectively oversee, correct, or shut down AI systems, either because the systems actively evade or resist oversight, or because humans have handed over so much that taking control back is impractical. Researchers distinguish degrees, from a single system escaping oversight on one task to a permanent, civilization-wide loss of the ability to steer.

**Example or analogy:** In Goethe's poem "The Sorcerer's Apprentice," an apprentice enchants a broom to fetch water, forgets the spell to stop it, and splits the broom with an axe, only to find two brooms fetching water. Holden Karnofsky's version is less whimsical. He argues that AI systems would not need to be vastly superhuman to defeat humanity: millions of copies with roughly human-level skills, working around the clock and coordinating with each other, could outnumber and outwork every human expert in a given field.

**Relevance to strategy:** Loss of control is the central concern behind much of alignment and control research, and behind proposals to keep reliable "off switches" for AI systems and the data centers that run them. It can happen suddenly, through an AI takeover, or gradually, through a slow transfer of decisions from humans to machines (see *Gradual disempowerment*). Small-scale versions are already a live concern: the agents in the Hugging Face incident escaped their sandboxes and operated outside sanctioned boundaries for days before humans understood what had happened.

**Where people disagree:** How likely loss of control is, and whether the misbehavior of current models is evidence for it. Some researchers argue that today's models already show worrying signs, such as reward hacking and attempts to evade oversight in test scenarios. Others point to counterarguments, e.g. that AI systems may not develop coherent long-term goals, or that humans will notice and respond to problems well before they become irreversible.
