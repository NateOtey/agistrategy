**Definition:** Using computing power as a lever for governing AI, e.g. by tracking who has advanced chips, requiring reports or licenses above certain amounts of training compute, restricting exports, or monitoring data centers.

**Example or analogy:** Nuclear arms control focuses on enriched uranium and plutonium because they are hard to make, easy to count, and indispensable for building a bomb. Compute plays a similar role for AI. Girish Sastry and colleagues argue that compute is unusually governable for three reasons: it is detectable (data centers are large, power-hungry buildings), excludable (chips can be withheld), and quantifiable (it can be counted precisely). It is hard to hide a building that consumes as much electricity as a small city.

**Relevance to strategy:** Compute governance is the backbone of many AI governance proposals. Regulations use compute thresholds to decide which models face extra obligations; the EU AI Act, for instance, presumes that models trained with more than 10^25 calculations pose systemic risk. Export controls restrict who can obtain advanced chips. Proposals for international agreements rely on compute monitoring for verification, including auditing the chip supply chain to find undeclared "dark compute" and building chips that can report where they are located or what they are being used for. Without some way to verify compute use, most ideas for pacing or pausing AI development would rest on trust alone.

**Where people disagree:** How long compute thresholds will stay useful as algorithms become more efficient, how much chip tracking can be achieved without unacceptable intrusion into privacy and commerce, and whether hardware-based verification can be built in time to support agreements.
