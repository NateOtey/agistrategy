**What it is:** A nonprofit that runs the field's most common entry point: free, cohort-based online courses in AGI strategy, frontier AI governance, technical AI safety and biosecurity, each pairing weekly readings with small facilitated discussions. It grew out of a Cambridge reading group started in 2021 and is now headquartered in San Francisco. It has trained more than 10,000 people, now trains more than 1,000 a month, and has taken over Coefficient Giving's career transition grants, deploying more than $1 million a month. It has raised $71 million, from Coefficient Giving.

**What it wants:** For the most important problems in AI safety and biosecurity to have "owners": capable people working on them full-time who build teams and raise money to solve them. Co-founder Dewi Erwan [argues](https://blog.bluedot.org/p/owners) that the field's bottleneck is talent rather than money, since only a few thousand people work full-time on making AI go well while philanthropies struggle to spend tens of billions of dollars fast enough.

**Resources and levers:** The front door. Its AGI Strategy course is the default first step that other organizations point newcomers to, so its reading lists shape what thousands of people first read when they take the field seriously. Selection: it decides who gets into courses, in-person programs and grants, and so who gets an early boost. A pipeline behind the courses: in-person programs, grants of $100 to $200,000, and a planned San Francisco campus for more than 200 people. Its alumni work at Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI and the UK AI Security Institute; by its count, about 1,200 of more than 9,000 course completers are now in roles it considers impactful.

**Obstacles and opponents:** Scale. Demand for the AGI Strategy course quadrupled in the first half of 2026, and keeping quality high is hard when much of the experience depends on small discussion groups and on the facilitator each person happens to get. Critics who argue that the field has more aspiring entrants than jobs, so that urging people to pivot can do them harm (see *Talent pipelines and field-building*). The same concerns that follow other parts of this network: reliance on a single large funder, ties to effective altruism, and the risk of helping to staff the labs whose race it worries about. Measuring its own impact, since it is hard to know who would have entered the field anyway.
