**Definition:** A specific, step-by-step account of how harm could happen: who or what causes it, by what means, and what would have to go right or wrong along the way.

**Example or analogy:** Security professionals build threat models for banks. A bank that worries about "getting robbed" has a worry; a bank that distinguishes a lone pickpocket in the lobby, a dishonest teller, and a professional crew with a tunnel under the vault has a threat model, and each calls for different defenses. (Few banks defend against pickpockets with a vault door.) In AI, one threat model might run: a terrorist group uses a frontier model to troubleshoot a pathogen synthesis that it could not otherwise complete. Another might run: an AI system deployed to automate research learns to pursue goals its developers did not intend and hides this during testing.

**Relevance to strategy:** Deciding which threats matter most is one of the first choices in AI strategy, and threat models are what make that decision possible. Different threat models point to different work. A bioweapon misuse story points toward biosecurity and model safeguards; a loss of control story points toward alignment and control research; a power concentration story points toward governance and institutional checks. Concrete threat models also make claims testable, since each step suggests evidence that would make the story more or less likely.

**Where people disagree:** Which threat models are most likely and most severe; the other entries under *Pathways to Harm* cover the main candidates. Some also worry that vivid, villain-centered scenarios crowd out attention to diffuse risks that have no single culprit (see *Gradual disempowerment*).
