**Definition:** A belief that, if it changed, would change your conclusion. A double crux is a belief that two people who disagree would both treat as decisive, so that settling it could resolve their disagreement.

**Example or analogy:** Two people disagree about whether governments should pause frontier AI development. After an hour of argument, they discover that both would favor a pause if they believed takeoff would be fast, and both would oppose one if they believed it would be slow. Takeoff speed is their double crux, and the productive next step is to look at evidence about takeoff speed instead of repeating arguments about pauses.

**Relevance to strategy:** Most disagreement about AI strategy rests on a handful of cruxes: how soon transformative AI will arrive, how fast takeoff will be, how hard alignment is, whether offense or defense will dominate, and how far to trust governments versus companies. Identifying your own cruxes tells you which evidence to watch and when to change course. Identifying other people's cruxes turns camp warfare into tractable questions and makes it possible to work with people who reach different conclusions. Cruxes also guide research priorities: the most valuable research often targets questions on which many people's strategies depend.

**Where people disagree:** Whether disagreements about AI reduce to a few factual cruxes, or whether they also reflect differences in values, temperament, and trust that no single piece of evidence will settle.
