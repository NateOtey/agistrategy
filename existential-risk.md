title: Existential vs. catastrophic risk
short: Existential risk

**Definition:** A catastrophic risk threatens harm on an enormous scale, e.g. millions of deaths or the collapse of critical global systems, from which civilization could eventually recover. An existential risk threatens human extinction, or the permanent and drastic loss of humanity's potential, for example through an unrecoverable collapse or a global tyranny that can never be overturned.

**Example or analogy:** The philosopher Derek Parfit asked readers to compare three outcomes: peace, a nuclear war that kills 99% of humanity, and a nuclear war that kills 100%. Most people assume the big gap lies between peace and the 99% war. Parfit argued that the gap between 99% and 100% is far greater, because the survivors of the first war could rebuild, and their descendants could live for millions of years, while the second war ends the story for good.

**Relevance to strategy:** The distinction shapes priorities. Those who weigh the interests of future generations heavily, often called longtermists, give special weight to existential risks because they foreclose everything that might come afterward. The distinction also matters for communication and coalition-building. In 2023, hundreds of AI researchers and executives signed a one-sentence statement that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Policymakers, by contrast, often respond more readily to catastrophic framings, such as AI-enabled cyberattacks on hospitals, that do not require accepting arguments about extinction.

**Where people disagree:** Whether talk of extinction distracts from present and catastrophic harms, or whether it is the necessary frame for risks of that size. There is also debate about how recoverable various catastrophes would be, which determines where the line between the categories falls in practice.
