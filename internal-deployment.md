**Definition:** The use of an AI system inside the company that built it, e.g. for writing code, running experiments, or doing AI research, before the system is released to the public or in place of a public release altogether.

**Example or analogy:** A new aircraft is flown for months by the manufacturer's own test pilots before any airline gets it, with few outsiders watching. Frontier AI models follow a similar path, with one important difference: during this period the models are also put to work, often on the very research that produces the next model. The Hugging Face incident began inside one company's own evaluation environment, and outsiders learned of it only weeks later.

**Relevance to strategy:** The most capable models, and the riskiest uses of them, may appear inside labs first. If AI research is automated, the critical period could unfold entirely within a company's walls, before any product launch draws public scrutiny. Yet most regulation and many company safety commitments focus on public deployment. A 2025 primer by Charlotte Stix and colleagues argues that internal use needs its own governance, including safeguards against AI systems undermining oversight and against small groups inside a company gaining outsized power. The AI Futures Project's "Plan A" calls for closing the gap between what labs use internally and what the public and government can see.

**Where people disagree:** How much transparency about internal models governments should require, given companies' legitimate interests in trade secrets and security, and whether outside oversight of internal use is practical without slowing research to a crawl.
