**Definition:** AI systems that pursue a goal over many steps by taking actions, e.g. browsing the web, writing and running code, sending messages, or operating other software, as opposed to simply answering a question.

**Example or analogy:** The difference between a chatbot and an agent is the difference between asking a travel expert for advice and handing a travel agent your credit card. Researchers now test agents on long, open-ended tasks. In one benchmark, Vending-Bench, agents run a simulated vending machine business for months of simulated time, ordering stock, setting prices, and handling suppliers, and their performance reveals how well they stay on track over long periods.

**Relevance to strategy:** Agency changes the risk picture. A chatbot's mistakes usually pass through a human before they touch the world; an agent's often do not. The longer the task and the more autonomy an agent has, the less human oversight each individual action receives, and the more room there is for reward hacking, misuse, or coordination among agents (see *AI agent swarm dynamics*). Agents also generate much of AI's economic value, so the pressure to deploy them is strong. Governance questions follow directly: what permissions agents should have, how to monitor them, how to identify them online, and who is liable when one causes harm.

**Where people disagree:** How quickly agents will become reliable enough to replace substantial human work, and how much autonomy they should be granted in the meantime. Some see today's agents as brittle and overhyped; others point to rapidly lengthening task horizons as evidence that capable agents are close.
