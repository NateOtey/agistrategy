**Definition:** A capabilities externality is the extent to which work done in the name of safety also makes AI systems more capable, and so speeds up the race it was meant to make safer. Safety-washing is presenting work, a product or a company as safety-focused when its main effect is on capabilities or reputation.

**Example or analogy:** Reinforcement learning from human feedback was developed partly as alignment research, and it is also what made chatbots useful enough to launch ChatGPT and set off the current race. Paul Christiano, one of its inventors, has argued that the research was still net positive while acknowledging the acceleration. In 2024, researchers found that many popular AI safety benchmarks correlate strongly with general capability, so that a lab can appear to make "safety progress" simply by building a bigger model. Think of a car company that advertises its safety record mainly by citing how fast its cars can brake from 200 miles an hour.

**Relevance to strategy:** Anyone choosing what to work on has to ask whether their work could make things worse. The question is sharpest for jobs at frontier labs, where safety teams improve the systems that are actually being deployed but also lend credibility, talent and sometimes techniques to the company's push toward more capable systems. It also matters for funders and evaluators deciding which research counts as safety at all.

**Where people disagree:** Whether working at a frontier lab is, on balance, good for safety. Supporters argue that the most important safety work requires access to frontier models and that safety-minded staff improve their employers' decisions; critics argue that labs use safety staff as credibility while the race continues, and that the best safety researchers would do more good holding labs accountable from outside. People also disagree about how to measure whether a given research agenda advances safety faster than capabilities.
