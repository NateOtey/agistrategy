**Definition:** The relative ease of attacking versus defending in a given domain. When offense has the advantage, a small investment in attack can overwhelm a large investment in defense; when defense has the advantage, the reverse holds.

**Example or analogy:** For centuries, a well-built castle could hold off armies many times the size of its garrison: defense dominated. Gunpowder artillery changed the balance, and within a few generations castle walls had become more decorative than protective. AI may shift balances in a similar way. In biology, a single engineered pathogen could spread worldwide faster than vaccines can be developed and distributed to billions of people, which looks like an offense-dominant domain. In cybersecurity, AI that finds software vulnerabilities helps attackers exploit them and helps defenders patch them, and it is not yet clear which side benefits more. (Castle architects, to their credit, adapted; they just stopped calling the results castles.)

**Relevance to strategy:** The offense–defense balance underlies several major debates. If AI favors offense, widely distributed capabilities are dangerous, which strengthens the case for restricting access, securing model weights, and keeping powerful systems in few hands. If AI favors defense, or can be made to, then openness and decentralization look safer, which is the hope behind d/acc. Toby Shevlane and Allan Dafoe have applied the same question to scientific publishing, asking when sharing AI research helps defenders more than attackers.

**Where people disagree:** Which way AI tips the balance in each domain, and whether deliberate investment in defense can change it. Optimists point to AI-powered defenses in cybersecurity and biosurveillance; pessimists note that defenders must succeed everywhere while an attacker needs to succeed only once.

**Related terms:** Dual use · Differential technological development and d/acc · Open vs. closed weights · Unipolar vs. multipolar
