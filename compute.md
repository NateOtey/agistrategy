**Definition:** The raw computing power used to train and run AI models. It is usually measured by the number of calculations performed (in units called FLOP) and supplied by specialized chips housed in large data centers.

**Example or analogy:** Compute is to AI roughly what fuel is to rocketry. A better engine design gets more out of every liter, but nobody reaches orbit on a thimbleful. Epoch AI estimates that the compute used to train frontier models has grown by roughly four to five times every year for over a decade. That is the difference between a garden hose and Niagara Falls, reached in about a dozen years.

**Relevance to strategy:** Compute is the most measurable and controllable input to AI progress. Algorithms and data can be copied and shared; chips are physical objects made by a handful of companies. One Taiwanese firm, TSMC, fabricates most advanced AI chips, using machines that only one Dutch company, ASML, can build, and the chips sit in buildings that draw as much electricity as small cities. This makes compute the natural lever for governance: export controls, reporting thresholds, and proposals for verifying international agreements all start here. Compute is also a key quantity for forecasting, since much of the case for near-term AGI rests on extrapolating how much computing power labs will be able to buy and build.

**Where people disagree:** How much progress depends on compute versus better algorithms and data. If algorithmic efficiency keeps improving quickly, the same capabilities may soon require far less compute, which would weaken compute-based governance. There is also debate about how long the growth can continue before running into limits on power, chip supply, or money.
