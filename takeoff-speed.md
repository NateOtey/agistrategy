**Definition:** How quickly AI goes from roughly human-level ability to vastly superhuman ability. A "slow" or "soft" takeoff unfolds over years or decades; a "fast" or "hard" takeoff happens over months, weeks, or less.

**Example or analogy:** Intuition pump: a slow takeoff is a sunrise, and a fast takeoff is someone flipping on the light switch. During a sunrise, everyone can see what is coming, adjust their plans, and notice problems while there is still time to fix them. The researcher Paul Christiano offered a concrete test for a slow takeoff: the world economy doubles in size over four years before it ever doubles in a single year.

**Relevance to strategy:** Takeoff speed determines how much warning the world gets and whether humanity can learn from mistakes as it goes. A slow takeoff favors building institutions that can respond to problems as they appear; a fast takeoff means most of the important work has to be done beforehand. Takeoff speed also shapes the balance of power: in a fast takeoff, whoever crosses the threshold first may pull far ahead (see *Unipolar vs. multipolar*). Some strategic choices can change the speed itself. Tom Davidson warns, for example, that pausing algorithmic progress while computing power keeps accumulating could create "dry tinder" for a faster takeoff if the pause ever breaks down.

**Where people disagree:** This is one of the field's oldest disputes, famously debated by Eliezer Yudkowsky, who expected a fast takeoff, and Paul Christiano, who expected a slower one. Much hinges on how strong the feedback loop from automated AI research turns out to be, and on how much real-world bottlenecks slow it down.
