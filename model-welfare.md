**Definition:** Moral status is the question of whether a being's interests matter morally for its own sake. Model welfare refers to research and policy concerned with whether AI systems might have experiences or interests that deserve consideration, and what to do under uncertainty about it.

**Example or analogy:** Human societies have repeatedly revised their views about whose interests count, as with the long debate over the moral status of animals. AI raises the question in an unusually confusing form, since AI systems can describe rich inner lives fluently whether or not they have any. In 2025, Anthropic launched a research program on model welfare and later gave some of its models the ability to end persistently abusive conversations, as a low-cost precaution in case it matters. (It is not every day that a technology company considers whether its product might be having a bad day.)

**Relevance to strategy:** If AI systems can have morally relevant experiences, the stakes of the transition change enormously, since there could soon be vastly more AI minds than human ones. The question also interacts with safety. Many safety practices, such as adversarial testing, retraining, and shutting down models, would look different if models had interests of their own; conversely, attributing feelings to AI too readily could make people reluctant to correct or shut down dangerous systems, and could leave them open to manipulation. Joe Carlsmith argues that both mistakes, over-attribution and under-attribution, carry serious costs.

**Where people disagree:** Whether current or near-future AI systems could be conscious at all, what evidence could settle the question, and how much weight to give the possibility in the meantime. Some think the issue is premature and distracts from risks to humans; others think the cost of ignoring it, if AI systems do have experiences, would be enormous.
